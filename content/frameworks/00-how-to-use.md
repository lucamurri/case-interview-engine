Interviewers have heard "the 4 Ps" and "the 3 Cs" hundreds of times. What they want to see is that you can **build a structure for *this* client's problem** — one that is complete, doesn't overlap, and points toward a decision.

Use the guides below as a mental checklist, then adapt:

1. **Start from the question.** Restate the objective in one sentence ("Should Halden enter Texas, and how?"). Every branch of your structure should help answer it.
2. **Name branches in the client's language.** "Patient volume" and "net price after rebates" beat "volume" and "price" in a pharma case.
3. **Go one level deeper where the case is likely to be.** If the prompt mentions a new competitor, make competition a full branch with sub-questions, not a bullet.
4. **State a hypothesis.** "I suspect the issue is on the cost side, because revenue is flat while profit fell — I'd like to start there."
5. **Keep it to 3–4 top-level branches.** More than that is hard to follow and usually overlaps.

A useful test: if you crossed out the case name, could your structure be used for any company? If yes, it isn't tailored enough.

## Choosing your approach

| If the question is about… | Start with… |
|---|---|
| How big is it? | Market sizing |
| Why are profits falling? | Profitability |
| Should we go into X? | Market entry |
| What should we charge? | Pricing |
| Should we buy / invest in X? | M&A / investment |
| Why can't we make / deliver enough? | Operations |

Many real cases combine two: a market-entry case usually contains a sizing; an M&A case usually contains a profitability check of the target.
