---
title: "3. How do we cut costs?"
order: 3
summary: "Find where the money goes first, then attack the biggest buckets with the right lever."
---

## Use it when

A client must reduce costs: margins are under pressure, a budget target is set ("take out 15%"), or a competitor is structurally cheaper.

## The core logic

1. **Map the cost base.** Break total cost into its biggest buckets (by function, by product, or by cost type such as people, materials, logistics and IT). Look for the few buckets that make up most of the total.
2. **For each big bucket, ask three questions:**
   - **Price paid:** are we paying too much per unit? (Procurement, contract terms, supplier mix, location.)
   - **Quantity used:** do we use too much? (Specifications, waste, demand that could be reduced.)
   - **How the work is done:** could it be done differently? (Process redesign, automation, outsourcing, footprint consolidation.)
3. **Benchmark** against competitors or best-performing sites to size what's realistic.
4. **Protect what customers value.** Separate cuts that customers won't notice from cuts that could cost revenue.
5. **Phase it:** quick wins versus structural changes, one-off savings versus recurring ones, and the cost to implement.

## Questions that unlock it

- How big is the target, and by when?
- Which costs are fixed and which are variable? Which have grown fastest?
- Are there constraints: unions, regulation, quality standards?

## Adapt it

- **Pharma / manufacturing:** materials and batch yield, plant utilization, network consolidation. Quality and regulatory compliance are non-negotiable.
- **Hospitals:** staffing mix and scheduling, supply standardization, length of stay.
- **Tech:** cloud and AI compute spend (right-sizing, model routing), vendor consolidation.
- **Retail:** store labor scheduling, shrink and waste, last-mile delivery.

## Traps

- Jumping to layoffs as the first idea.
- Cutting across the board instead of where the money actually is.
- Counting one-off savings as recurring.

## Coach's note

Lead with the **cost map**. "Before suggesting cuts, I'd like to know where the money goes: what are the five biggest cost lines?" That question alone signals seniority. Then size each idea so the savings add up to the target.
